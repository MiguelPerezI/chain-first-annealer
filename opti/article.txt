Abstract

We describe and implement a randomized algorithm that inputs a polyhedron, thought of as the space of states of some automated guided vehicle ℛ\mathcal{R}, and outputs an explicit system of piecewise linear motion planners for ℛ\mathcal{R}. The algorithm is designed in such a way that the cardinality of the outputed system is probabilistically close (with parameters chosen by the user) to minimal possible. This yields the first automated solution for robust-to-noise robot motion planning in terms of simplicial complexity (SC) techniques, a discretization of Farber’s topological complexity TC\mathrm{TC}. Besides its relevance toward technological applications, our work revels that, unlike other discrete approaches to TC, the SC model can recast Farber’s invariant without having to introduce costly subdivisions. We develop and implement our algorithm by actually discretizing Macías-Virgós and Mosquera-Lois’ notion of homotopic distance, thus encompassing computer estimations of other sectional category invariants as well, such as the Lusternik-Schnirelmann category of polyhedra.

Keywords and phrases: Abstract simplicial complex, barycentric subdivision, contiguity of simplicial maps, motion planning, randomized algorithm, homotopic distance.

1 Introduction

The notion of topological complexity (TC), introduced by Michael Farber in [4], is a mathematical model measuring the continuity instabilities in the motion planning problem of robots. The theoretical aspects of Farber’s idea have been extensively studied by algebraic topologists through nearly twenty years. As a result, the concept has found deep and fruitful connections in homotopy theory. Nonetheless, the more computational aspects of the TC-ideas have seen limited developments, while actual engineering-minded TC-applications seem to be inexistent. The purpose of this paper is to help mend such a situation by focusing on the more applied features of Farber’s TC. We take advantage of computational topology techniques and, more importantly, by using randomized algorithms, we settle a cumputer-based solution to the motion planning problem for autonomous systems under Farber’s model. We implement our algorithms with successful and, as explained below, unexpected results.

We use the discretization of Farber’s TC developed in [7]. Here is the basic idea (details are reviewed and generalized in the next section). Assume that the space of states of a given autonomous system is given by (the topological realization of) an abstract simplicial complex KK where, for practical reasons, we assume KK to be finite. Taking a linear order on the vertices of KK, we consider the ordered simplicial product K×KK\times K. In such a context, a piecewise linear motion planner defined on a subcomplex JJ of K×KK\times K is a chain of simplicial maps φ0,φ1,…,φc:J→K\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{c}\colon J\to K (c≥1c\geq 1) satisfying that (i) each φi\varphi_{i} is contiguous to the subsequent map φi+1\varphi_{i+1} (0≤i<c0\leq i<c), and that (ii) φ0\varphi_{0} (φc\varphi_{c}) is the restriction to JJ of the projection K×K→KK\times K\to K onto the first (second) factor. The rationale behind such a definition is that, for any given pair (a,b)(a,b) of initial-final points in the topological realization ‖J‖\|J\| of JJ, the sequence of points

a,‖φ0‖​(a,b),‖φ1‖​(a,b),…,‖φc‖​(a,b),ba,\;\|\varphi_{0}\|(a,b),\;\|\varphi_{1}\|(a,b),\,\ldots\,,\;\|\varphi_{c}\|(a,b),\;b

flags a piecewise linear path in ‖K‖\|K\| from aa to bb depending continuously on aa and bb. A motion planner defined on the full complex K×KK\times K exists only in the (topologically trivial) case that ‖K‖\|K\| is contractible. It is then natural to define SCstrict​(K)\mathrm{SC}_{\text{strict}}(K) as one less than the minimal cardinality of systems of piecewise linear motion planners whose domains cover K×KK\times K. This yields a discretized approximation of Farber’s TC in the sense that TC⁡(‖K‖)≤SCstrict​(K)\mathrm{TC}(\|K\|)\leq\mathrm{SC}_{\text{strict}}(K).

A central result in [7] (coming from the Simplicial Approximation Theorem) is that, if we want to have a discretized invariant of KK that actually recovers (rather than just approximates) Farber’s topological complexity of the realization ‖K‖\|K\|, then the above construction has to be done within a limiting process where K×KK\times K is allowed to be “sufficiently” subdivided, for instance, by taking bb-iterated barycentric subdivisions Sdb​(K×K)\mathrm{Sd}^{b}(K\times K). This yields the simplicial complexity (SC) of KK,

SC⁡(K)=limb→∞SCstrictb​(K),\mathrm{SC}(K)=\lim_{b\to\infty}\mathrm{SC}^{\hskip 0.7113ptb}_{\text{strict}}(K),

where SCstrictb​(K)\mathrm{SC}^{\hskip 0.7113ptb}_{\text{strict}}(K) is defined in terms of Sdb​(K×K)\mathrm{Sd}^{b}(K\times K) in the same way as SCstrict​(K)\mathrm{SC}_{\text{strict}}(K) is defined in terms of K×KK\times K (so SCstrict0​(K)=SCstrict​(K)\mathrm{SC}^{0}_{\text{strict}}(K)=\mathrm{SC}_{\text{strict}}(K)). Then, as shown in [7, Theorem 3.5],

SCstrict0​(K×K)≥SCstrict1​(K×K)≥SCstrict2​(K×K)≥⋯≥SC⁡(K)=TC⁡(‖K‖),\mathrm{SC}^{0}_{\text{strict}}(K\times K)\geq\mathrm{SC}^{1}_{\text{strict}}(K\times K)\geq\mathrm{SC}^{2}_{\text{strict}}(K\times K)\geq\cdots\geq\mathrm{SC}(K)=\mathrm{TC}(\|K\|),

(1)

recovering Farber’s topological complexity of ‖K‖\|K\|. Our main theoretical result, Theorem 3.3 below, is a generalization of (1). This is achieved by introducing the notion of contiguity distance between simplicial maps (Definition 3.2), a discretization of the concept of homotopic distance studied recently in [8].

Remark 1.1.

The point above is that, by taking iterated barycentric subdivisions, we can recast Farber’s invariant by means of a finite model. However, any person familiar with the computational aspects involving subdivisions will immediately realize that the proposal based on bb-iterated barycentric subdivisions becomes computationally prohibitive as bb grows. Thus, in principle, the use of SC to evaluate TC carries a compromise between how close we want to approximate TC\mathrm{TC} versus how long we are willing to wait for a computer to perform a calculation involving a large number of subdivisions. The following considerations explain that, in some cases, such compromise can be minimized without diminishing the effectiveness of the approach.

An initial way to soften the compromise above is to allow for coarser/partial subdivisions. The option is however still not satisfactory, as there is no indication a priori of what portions of K×KK\times K (and how much) should be subdivided. With the aid of human geometric intuition, the coarser subdivision alternative was used in [7, Section 4] to recast, after a single subdivision, the fact that the topological complexity of the circle (the natural benchmark in [7]) is 1, namely,

SCstrictb​(∂Δ2)=1​,  for b≥1.\mathrm{SC}^{\hskip 0.56905ptb}_{\text{strict}}(\partial\Delta^{2})=1\mbox{, \ for $b\geq 1$.}

(2)

With these preliminaries, we are ready to describe the main application-minded achievements of this paper. Firstly, we lay the grounds for a randomized algorithm that is able to perform, without human aid, evaluation tasks such as (2). Secondly, and more relevant for actual applications, we set a corresponding computer implementation which, besides of performing automated TC estimations, produces probabilistically close-to-optimal systems of piecewise linear motion planners. Optimality here means that, in each inputed case, the cardinality of the outputed system is close to been minimal possible (i.e., close to the TC value of the complex under consideration), where closeness is controlled by means of user-given parameters in the probabilistic algorithm. In brief, our implementation outputs robust-to-noise system of piecewise linear motion planners, with a reliability that depends on the given parameters.

As a byproduct, we find somehow surprisingly that the subdivision ingredient implicit in (1) can be unnecessary in a SC-recasting of Farber’s TC, thus nullifying the critical compromise noted in Remark 1.1. This is a crucial feature for eventual technological TC-applications. For instance, running on a laptop computer, our algorithm finds in a bit less than a minute a system of piecewise linear motion planners giving in fact

SCstrict0​(∂Δ2)=1.\mathrm{SC}^{\hskip 0.56905pt0}_{\text{strict}}(\partial\Delta^{2})=1.

(3)

This should be compared to the fact that, starting with the barycentric subdivision Sd1​(∂Δ2×∂Δ2)\mathrm{Sd}^{1}(\partial\Delta^{2}\times\partial\Delta^{2}), our computer implementation finds in about 10 hours running time a system of piecewise linear motion planners recovering the weaker fact (2). The reported running times (obtained on the same laptop computer) are given for comparison purposes: the computation is about 600 times (unnecessarily) more complex by allowing a single subdivision. Naturally, the use of powerful computational clusters results in significant time reductions, thus allowing to replace the circle ∂Δ2\partial\Delta^{2} by complexes arising from actual applications. The moral is that, in general, an SC-approach to motion planning applications tends to perform satisfactorily well by ignoring the subdivision component. Actual applications of the SC-ideas to concrete problems by using powerful computer clusters will be addressed in a future publication.

Remark 1.2.

The ability to recast Farber’s TC in discrete terms without the need of subdivisions seems to be a convenient advantage of our SC-model over other discrete approaches. Specifically, the discrete topological complexity introduced in [6] of a simplicial complex whose geometric realization is a circle turns out to be one unit larger than TC⁡(S1)\mathrm{TC}(S^{1}) [6, Theorem 5.6]. Likewise, (if no subdivisions are allowed) the value of Tanaka’s combinatorial complexity on the minimal finite space model of a circle is two units higher than TC⁡(S1)\mathrm{TC}(S^{1}) ([10, Example 3.7]).

The first part of the paper is devoted to reviewing the basic tools (Section 2) and developping the theoretical basis (Section 3) that show that our model recasts homotopy notions such as Farber’s topological complexity and Lusternik-Schnirelmann’s category. This is done via Macías-Virgós and Mosquera-Lois’ homotopic distance, a concept whose discretization is achieved by means of Theorem 3.3. The second part of the paper has an applied focus: Section 4 describes the main processes in our algorithm, while Section 5 illustrates, via experimentation, the power that the technique has even when the subdivision ingredient is omitted.

2 Background

2.1 Topological complexity via homotopic distance

Let P⁡(X)P(X) stand for the free path space on a topological space XX. Farber’s topological complexity of XX, TC⁡(X)\mathrm{TC}(X), is the sectional category of the end-point evaluation map e:P⁡(X)→X×Xe\colon P(X)\to X\times X, i.e., the fibration taking a free path γ∈P⁡(X)\gamma\in P(X) to the pair (γ⁡(0),γ⁡(1))(\gamma(0),\gamma(1)). In other words, TC⁡(X)+1\mathrm{TC}(X)+1 is the smallest cardinality of open covers {Ui}i\{U_{i}\}_{i} of X×XX\times X so that ee admits a continuous section σi\sigma_{i} on each UiU_{i}. Note that we use reduced terms, so that a contractible space has zero topological complexity. The open sets UiU_{i} in such an open cover are called local domains, the corresponding sections σi\sigma_{i} are called local motion planners, and the family of pairs {(Ui,σi)}\{(U_{i},\sigma_{i})\} is called a system of local motion planners for XX. A system of local motion planners is said to be optimal if it has TC⁡(X)+1\mathrm{TC}(X)+1 local domains. In view of the continuity requirement on local rules, an optimal motion planner minimizes the possibility of accidents in the performance of a robot moving in a noisy environment.

When XX is the realization of an abstract simplicial complex, the openness requirement on local domains can be replaced, without altering the numerical value of TC⁡(X)\mathrm{TC}(X), by requiring that local domains are subdivided subcomplexes (see [7], or the adaptation to the simplicial analogue of homotopic distance in the proof of Theorem 3.3 below). Furthermore, a local motion planner is really a (local) homotopy in disguise:

Lemma 2.1 ([5, Lemma 4.21]).

The evaluation map e:P⁡(X)→X×Xe\colon P(X)\to X\times X admits a section on a subset AA of X×XX\times X if and only if the restrictions to AA of the two projections to the axes πi:X×X→X\pi_{i}\colon X\times X\to X, i=1,2i=1,2, are homotopic.

Consequently, TC⁡(X)=D⁡(p1,p2)\mathrm{TC}(X)=\mathrm{D}(p_{1},p_{2}), where:

Definition 2.2 ([8]).

The homotopic distance D⁡(f,g)\mathrm{D}(f,g) between two continuous maps f,g:X→Yf,g\colon X\to Y is one less than the minimal cardinality of covers of XX by open sets UU on each of which the restricted maps f|Uf_{|U} and g|Ug_{|U} are homotopic.

2.2 Contiguity vs. homotopy

For details on the facts reviewed in this subsection, the reader can consult standard references, such as [9, Chapter 3].

Definition 2.3.

For a positive integer cc, and a pair of simplicial maps φ,φ′:J→K\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}:J\to K,

1. 

φ\varphi and φ′\varphi^{\prime} are said to be 1-contiguous provided φ⁡(σ)∪φ′​(σ)\varphi(\sigma)\cup\varphi^{\prime}(\sigma) is a simplex of KK for any facet (i.e., maximal simplex) σ\sigma of JJ.

2. 

φ\varphi and φ′\varphi^{\prime} are said to be cc-contiguous if there is a sequence of maps φ0,φ1,⋯,φc:J→K\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},\cdots,\varphi_{c}:J\to K, with φ0=φ\varphi_{0}=\varphi and φc=φ′\varphi_{c}=\varphi^{\prime}, such that φi−1\varphi_{i-1} and φi\varphi_{i} are 1-contiguous for each i∈{1,2,…,c}i\in\{1,2,\ldots,c\}.

The sequence of maps φj\varphi_{j} in Definition 2.3.2 is called a contiguity chain of length cc between φ\varphi and φ′\varphi^{\prime}. We write φ∼cφ′\varphi\sim_{c}\varphi^{\prime} to mean that there is such a sequence, and write φ∼φ′\varphi\sim\varphi^{\prime} to mean φ∼cφ′\varphi\sim_{c}\varphi^{\prime} for some cc. The latter is an equivalence relation in the set of simplicial maps J→KJ\to K. The corresponding equivalence classes are called contiguity classes. Note that contiguity classes are preserved under composition.

Remark 2.4.

Contiguity classes refine homotopy classes in the sense that the topological realizations of a pair of contiguous simplicial maps are homotopic through a piecewise affine homotopy. More importantly, as soon as highly subdivided complexes are allowed, this leads to a one-to-one correspondence between contiguity and homotopy classes. To make the latter statement precise (in Theorem 2.6 below), we need:

Definition 2.5.

(a) For a simplex σ\sigma of KK, the open realized simplex ⟨σ⟩⊆‖K‖\langle\sigma\rangle\subseteq\|K\| consists of the barycentric combinations ∑v∈σtv​v\sum_{v\in\sigma}t_{v}v with each tvt_{v} positive. Note that the realization of σ\sigma, ‖σ‖⊆‖K‖\|\sigma\|\subseteq\|K\|, is the closure of ⟨σ⟩\langle\sigma\rangle. (b) A simplicial approximation of a continuous map f:‖J‖→‖K‖f\colon\|J\|\to\|K\| is a simplicial map φ:J→K\varphi\colon J\to K such that ‖φ‖​(x)∈‖σ‖\|\varphi\|(x)\in\|\sigma\| provided f⁡(x)∈⟨σ⟩f(x)\in\langle\sigma\rangle.

For instance, a simplicial map is the only approximation of its geometric realization. On the other hand, for a subdivision K′K^{\prime} of KK, the standard homeomorphism ‖K′‖→=‖K‖\|K^{\prime}\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle=}}{{\to}}\|K\| is approximated by any simplicial map φ:K′→K\varphi\colon K^{\prime}\to K with the property that, for every vertex v′v^{\prime} of K′K^{\prime}, the barycentric coordinate of v′∈‖K‖v^{\prime}\in\|K\| at φ⁡(v′)\varphi(v^{\prime}) is positive. Further, simplicial composition of approximations approximates the corresponding topological composition.

Theorem 2.6.

Uniqueness of approximations: Any two approximations of a continuous map are 11-contiguous. Further, for finite JJ:

(i) 

Existence of approximations: Any continuous map f:‖J‖→‖K‖f\colon\|J\|\to\|K\| admits an approximation φb:Sdb​(J)→K\varphi_{b}\colon\mathrm{Sd}^{b}(J)\to K for any bb large enough. In particular, if ι:Sdb+1​(J)→Sdb​(J)\iota\colon\mathrm{Sd}^{b+1}(J)\to\mathrm{Sd}^{b}(J) is any approximation of the identity on ‖J‖\|J\|, then φb​ι\varphi_{b}\hskip 0.85358pt\iota and φb+1\varphi_{b+1} are 1-contiguous.

(ii) 

Contiguity vs. homotopy: Recall that simplicial maps in the same contiguity class have homotopic topological realizations (Remark 2.4). Conversly, given homotopic maps f0,f1:‖J‖→‖K‖f_{0},f_{1}\colon\|J\|\to\|K\|, there is a non-negative integer b0b_{0} such that, for each b≥b0b\geq b_{0}, any pair of approximations φ0,φ1:Sdb​(J)→K\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1}\colon\mathrm{Sd}^{b}(J)\to K of f0,f1f_{0},f_{1}, respectively, satisfy φ0∼φ1\varphi_{0}\sim\varphi_{1}.

All complexes we deal with will be assumed to be finite. Additionally whenever we need to talk about products, they will be taken in the category of ordered complexes (fixing some linear order on the corresponding sets of vertices). The key point is that the topological realization of an ordered product of complexes is homeomorphic to the product of the topological realizations of the factors (see [3]). We do not require, though, that maps of ordered complexes preserve the given orderings.

3 Contiguity distance

We now adapt the viewpoint in [7] in order to recast, in simplicial terms, the homotopic distance between continuous functions. Throughout this section φ,φ′:L→K\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}\colon L\to K denote a fixed pair of simplicial maps. A subcomplex JJ of LL is said to be a cc-contiguity subcomplex for φ\varphi and φ′\varphi^{\prime} if the restrictions φ|J\varphi_{|J} and φ′|J\varphi^{\prime}_{|J} are cc-contiguous. We say that JJ is a contiguity subcomplex for φ\varphi and φ′\varphi^{\prime} if it is a cc-contiguity subcomplex for some cc. The strict cc-contiguity distance of φ\varphi and φ′\varphi^{\prime}, denoted by SDc​(φ,φ′)\mathrm{SD}_{c}(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}), is defined as one less than the smallest cardinality of finite covers of LL by cc-contiguity subcomplexes for φ\varphi and φ′\varphi^{\prime}. The strict contiguity distance of φ\varphi and φ′\varphi^{\prime} is SD⁡(φ,φ′):=limcSDc​(φ,φ′)\mathrm{SD}(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}):=\lim_{c}\mathrm{SD}_{c}(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}), the eventual constant value of the monotonic sequence

SD1​(φ,φ′)≥SD2​(φ,φ′)≥SD3​(φ,φ′)≥⋯.\mathrm{SD}_{1}(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})\geq\mathrm{SD}_{2}(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})\geq\mathrm{SD}_{3}(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})\geq\cdots.

The concept of strict contiguity distance was introduced in [8, Definition 8.1] (see also [2]) under the name of contiguity distance. We use the shorter name for the actual notion recovering homotopic distance (see Definition 3.2 and Theorem 3.3 below).

4 Algorithms

We now describe a randomized algorithm whose implementation yields reasonable estimations of the contiguity distance between simplicial maps. As described in the introductory section, in the case of simplicial complexity, our implementation constructs reasonably close-to-optimal systems of piecewise linear local motion planners.

We represent an (abstract simplicial) complex KK by a pair (VK,FK)(V_{K},F_{K}), where VKV_{K} is the list of the vertices of KK, and FKF_{K} is the list of the facets (i.e. maximal simplices) of KK. This encoding is convenient because of Definition 2.3.1 and, more importantly for our purposes, since a covering of a given complex is completely determined by a covering of its facets (see the discussion at the beginning of Subsection 4.3).

In what follows we assume given an algorithm, Contiguous, that checks whether two simplicial maps are contiguous11

4.1 Randomized local search of contiguity chains

Recall that, for finite abstract simplicial complexes JJ and KK, Theorem 2.6 characterizes homotopy classes of continuous maps ‖J‖→‖K‖\|J\|\to\|K\| in terms of contiguity classes of maps J→KJ\to K through a limiting process that takes finer and finer subdivisions of JJ. This classical fact is generalized in [1] by introducing a certain “contiguity complex” Contig​(J,K)\text{Contig}(J,K) that approximates, as JJ becomes sufficiently subdivided, the homotopy type of the function space of continuous maps ‖J‖→‖K‖\|J\|\to\|K\|. What is relevant for us is to remark that Blumberg and Mandell propose a certain randomized algorithm in order to study the rate of growth (under subdivision of the domain) of the components of Contig​(J,K)\text{Contig}(J,K). We adapt Blumberg-Mandell’s algorithm (without the subdivision component) for our computer implemented search of piecewise linear local motion planners.

We start with the algorithm AddFacet, whose input is a pair of simplicial maps ψ,ψ′:L→K\psi,\psi^{\prime}\colon L\to K, and a contiguity subcomplex JJ for ψ\psi and ψ′\psi^{\prime}. The main algorithm in this subsection, RCC, is an iteration of AddFacet. Starting with two simplicial maps ψ,ψ′:L→K\psi,\psi^{\prime}\colon L\to K as input, RCC applies AddFacet recursively, using the output of the previous application as (part of) the input for the next application (ψ\psi and ψ′\psi^{\prime} are kept as the rest of the input for all iterations of AddFacet). The iteration is applied at most Card⁡(FL)\mathrm{Card}(F_{L}) times, and is set to stop whenever the current application of AddFacet is unable to add an additional facet (this will hold, for instance, if the total complex LL has been identified as a contiguity complex for ψ\psi and ψ′\psi^{\prime}).

A slight generalization of RCC will also be needed in what follows. The algorithm AddFacets is an iteration of AddFacet on the same grounds as RCC, except that the starting contiguity subcomplex is a prescribed parameter.

For latter use in the global algorithm, an actual implementation of both AddFacets and RCC should keep track of the (reduced version of the) last non-empty contiguity sequence ϕ\phi constructed by AddFacet, for this provides us with an explicit contiguity chain for the restrictions of ψ\psi and ψ′\psi^{\prime} to the output of AddFacets or RCC.
